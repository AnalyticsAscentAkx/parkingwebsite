What charging actually costs in the Netherlands

Public charging in the Netherlands is priced per kWh, and the price is set by whoever owns the post rather than by any national tariff. Across the 93,002 published prices in the national register, the middle of the market sits close to 41 cents.

€0.41 median price per kWh at a public charger. Most chargers fall between €0.28 and €0.63, and the national average is €0.43.

In practice that means charging a 60 kWh battery from 20 to 80 percent, which is about 36 kWh, costs roughly €15 at the median price. The same charge costs about €10 on the cheapest large network and €18 on the most expensive, before any parking is added.

The operator matters far more than the town. These are the average prices of the large networks, measured across every one of their locations:

The parking half of the bill

This is the part almost every charging map leaves out. A charging bay inside a paid parking zone is still a paid parking space. You pay the street tariff for the whole time you are plugged in, on top of the electricity.

12,667 charge points that sit inside a paid parking area, where the parking tariff is charged on top of the electricity.

For a short stop in an expensive city centre the parking can cost more than the charge. Two hours in central Amsterdam runs to about €7.50 in parking, against roughly €8 for 20 kWh of electricity. The map above adds both together for the arrival and departure time you choose, because the parking tariff changes with the hour and the day of the week. A stop that starts at 18:00 can be materially cheaper than the same stop at 16:00 at the very same bay.

If you want the parking side in detail, the street parking guide covers how Dutch zones and tariffs work, and free parking lists where you pay nothing at all.

How many chargers are broken right now

Operators publish the status of every charge point to the national register. Most of the time most of them work, but the failure rate is not evenly spread.

1.6% of Dutch charge points are reported out of order at this moment, about 3,194 of 201,286.

The national figure hides the thing that matters. Sorted by the share of their own points currently reported faulty, the gap between the best and worst large networks is about fifteenfold:

Fast charging is rarer than it looks

The Netherlands has the densest charging network in Europe by land area, but density is not the same as speed. The overwhelming majority of Dutch posts are ordinary AC chargers built for cars that sit still for hours.

2.6% of connectors deliver 150 kW or more. 94.6 percent are 11 to 22 kW AC posts.
  • 11 to 22 kW AC, 190,987 connectors. The standard street post. Adds roughly 60 to 100 km of range per hour.
  • 150 kW and above, 5,342 connectors. Motorway and hub charging, typically Fastned, Tesla, Allego or Shell Recharge.
  • 43 to 150 kW DC, 1,857 connectors. Less common than either extreme.
  • Under 11 kW, 3,721 connectors. Slow, often older kerbside posts.

If you need a genuine fast charge, use the Power sort on the map above. In a city like Leiden, which has 682 charging locations but almost no DC at all, the nearest real fast charger may be a motorway junction away.

Charging by city

Rotterdam and Den Haag both have more charging locations than Amsterdam, and the average price differs by city more than most drivers expect. Nijmegen averages €0.34 per kWh against Dordrecht at €0.52, a difference of about half again for the same energy.

Dutch charging words you will meet

Street signs, apps and parking meters are in Dutch. These are the words worth knowing.

Laadpaal
Charging post. A laadpaal usually serves two bays.
Laadpas
Charge card. The RFID card or app you hold against the post.
Snelladen
Fast charging, meaning DC rather than the usual AC post.
Laadtarief
The charging tariff, quoted per kWh.
Opladen
To charge. Opladen bezig means charging in progress.
Storing
Fault or outage. A post showing storing is broken.
Bezet
Occupied. Vrij means free and available.
Alleen voor opladen
EV charging only. Parking here without charging risks a fine.

Parking at a charging bay without actually charging is a ticketable offence in most municipalities. The parking fines guide covers what that costs and how to contest it.

Common questions

How much does it cost to charge an electric car in the Netherlands?

The median public price is €0.41 per kWh, with most chargers between €0.28 and €0.63. Charging a 60 kWh battery from 20 to 80 percent costs roughly €15 at the median. The operator matters more than the city: Vattenfall InCharge averages €0.32 per kWh, E-Flux by Road €0.51.

Do I pay for parking while charging?

Usually yes. At least 12,667 Dutch charge points sit inside a paid parking area, and the normal hourly tariff applies for the whole time you are plugged in. In a city centre the parking can easily exceed the cost of the electricity for a short stop. A handful of municipalities waive it, but that is the exception.

Which charge card is cheapest?

There is no single answer, which is the whole problem. Each card negotiates its own rate with each operator, so the cheapest card depends on whose post you are standing at. The spread between best and worst card at the same socket commonly reaches 40 to 70 percent. The map above shows the cheapest card for each location rather than recommending one card for everything.

How do I avoid arriving at a broken charger?

Check the operator-reported status before you drive. About 1.6 percent of Dutch charge points are reported out of order at any moment, but that ranges from under 1 percent on the best networks to over 16 percent on the worst. Red markers on the map above are points the operator itself is reporting as faulty.

Are there free charging stations in the Netherlands?

Effectively no. Only 52 of roughly 93,000 published tariffs list a zero price per kWh, and those are mostly at hotels, showrooms and workplaces rather than on the street. Charging outside paid parking hours is the closest thing to a saving, since the parking half of the bill drops to nothing.

Can I charge without a subscription or charge card?

Yes. Every public charger must offer ad-hoc payment, usually by contactless card or a QR code on the post. Ad-hoc is normally the most expensive way to pay, but for an occasional visitor it avoids a subscription entirely. The prices quoted on this page are the operators' ad-hoc rates.

Where are the fast chargers?

Only 2.6 percent of Dutch connectors deliver 150 kW or more. Amsterdam and Rotterdam have the most, over 200 points each, and the motorway network is well covered by Fastned, Tesla and Allego. Some sizeable cities, Leiden among them, have essentially none. Use the Power sort on the map to find them.

Where these numbers come from

Charging locations, connector specifications, prices and status come from the Dutch national charging register, published under the EU alternative fuels rules and refreshed continuously. Parking tariffs come from the national parking register and are matched to each charger by location, within 400 metres.

Prices are the operator's own ad-hoc rate per kWh. They exclude session fees, connection fees and whatever margin your charge card adds, all of which vary by card. Status is what the operator reports about its own equipment, so a post that fails without reporting it will still show as working. Figures on this page were computed from a full snapshot of the register and are refreshed as the data is rebuilt.
